The Breakthrough

Build without a ball. Golf's Fosbury Flop.

In 1968, deep foam landing pits replaced sand. Before foam, high jumpers used the “straddle” — facing the bar — because landing on your back in sawdust would break your spine. The straddle wasn't the best way to jump high. It was the best way to jump and survive the landing.

When foam arrived, Dick Fosbury realized the constraint had changed. He invented the “Fosbury Flop” — jumping backward, arched over the bar. Biomechanically superior, it won Olympic gold and rendered the old technique extinct within a decade.

For a hundred years golfers faced an impossible trade-off: keep the ball and get corrupted feedback while your brain overloads trying to hit a good shot and change mechanics at once — or remove the ball and swing blind. Video feedback changes the constraint. Pairing practice swings with instant playback, in a living room, lets you see your exact positions without the ball corrupting the experiment.

Golf finally has its foam pit.

The old constraint
A ball on the ground hijacks the working memory a swing change needs. You chase contact and abandon the build.
The new constraint
No ball. No target. No fear of a shank. Just the pattern, encoded piece by piece — verified on camera.

The paradigm shift

What fifty years of golf instruction got wrong.

Mainstream instruction treats golf like a mechanical problem. Science proves it's a motor-learning problem. Here are the false priors keeping golfers stuck.

What traditional instruction teaches
What the method rests on
Construction

Work on swing mechanics at the range, while hitting balls.

The Dual-Task Trap

The ball hijacks the working memory mechanics needs — building and hitting at once overloads both. Real construction happens closed-loop, without a ball.

CH. 02 · The Foam Pit
Construction

Build the backswing first, then the downswing.

Reverse Chaining

Rebuilding a swing — especially rewiring the pivot — isolate the most important part first: the downswing, not the backswing.

CH. 09 · Reverse Chaining
Construction

“Quiet feet” — stay grounded for control.

The Quiet-Feet Fallacy

Suppressing lower-body ground force kills power — tour players generate over 200% of body weight in vertical force through transition. They launch, not stay still.

CH. 11 · Ground Force
Construction

Build one swing with one pivot — the same motion for every club in the bag.

The Cannon & The Anchor

You need two pivots, not one. The Cannon — driver: jump and stall the hips to launch the clubhead. The Anchor — short irons and wedges: feet stay planted for a stable low point and control.

CH. 12 · Two Pivots
Calibration

A flush strike on the range means the change is working.

The Range Mirage

A good strike is often the old pattern lining up by luck. Real structural change produces a temporary, necessary dip in contact — the J-Curve.

CH. 07 · The Range Mirage
Transfer

Consistency comes fastest by repeating the same shot to the same target.

The Blocked-Practice Illusion

Blocked reps happen mostly Construction, not in transfer. Blocked reps feel confident and fail to transfer. Random targets and changing clubs build the “desirable difficulty” that actually sticks on the course. 

CH. 14 · Transfer
Performance

On-Course Reinvestment

Mechanical thought under real pressure consumes exactly the resources the shot needs, and performance collapses. Keep a firewall between garage mechanics and play. Simple external cues or well vetted feels ONLY. Don't experiment in compitition.

The Paradigm Shift

Twenty assumptions golf instruction runs on. All twenty, wrong.

A prior is an unconscious, stored prediction about what practice is, what a good swing looks like, or how learning works. They feel like common sense. Scientifically, most of them actively prevent motor learning. Every one below traces to a specific chapter and page in the book — check the work.

Read the book ›
Category A · Zone 01 · Construction

The Learning & Environment Priors

The Ball-Present Prior

The false prior

You must hit a golf ball to work on your swing mechanics.

The reality

A ball on the ground triggers outcome anxiety and pulls in survival wiring that hijacks the working memory mechanics needs. Rapid mechanical construction happens fastest closed-loop, without a ball at first.

Ch. 2, p. 32–34

The Top-Down Prior

The false prior

You build a swing from the top down — backswing, then transition, then downswing.

The reality

Under forward chaining, delivery geometry stays undefined, so the body improvises compensations mid-downswing. Reverse chaining builds the downswing and pivot first, giving the backswing a verified destination.

Ch. 9

The Ball-Flight Feedback Prior

The false prior

Where the ball goes during a mechanical change gives you useful feedback on whether the change worked.

The reality

In the Construction Zone, ball flight corrupts the experiment — a new pattern should look ugly at first, because the hands haven't tuned to the new delivery geometry yet.

Ch. 3, p. 50; Ch. 8, p. 185

The Immediate Outcome Prior (the Range Mirage)

The false prior

A well-struck, flush shot on the range means I'm learning.

The reality

A pure strike on the range is often the old, flawed swing lining up by luck for one rep. Real structural change causes a temporary, necessary decline in contact — the J-Curve.

Ch. 2, p. 36–40

The Slow-Motion Prior

The false prior

A slow-motion downswing is just a scaled-down version of a full-speed downswing.

The reality

Speed changes the physics — centrifugal force and motion-dependent torques only exist at full speed. Slow and full-speed swings are different gaits, like walking versus galloping. Swing the downswing hard, like you will on the course; slow the backswing instead, and that's what gives your brain the bandwidth to add downswing speed as it's ready.

Ch. 8

The Speed-After-Mechanics Prior

The false prior

Speed training has to wait until your mechanics are fully sound.

The reality

Speed and mechanics build together, not in sequence — through the same construction method. Freezers install the firing pattern first, then Smoothies add speed on the Tempo Ladder, exactly like every other Construction Zone skill.

Ch. 17

The Willpower Prior

The false prior

Failing to change your swing or stick to a practice routine is a failure of discipline and willpower.

The reality

Practice stagnation is a systems-design problem, not a grit problem — driven by the Competence Trap. It's solved by designing the environment and structure, not trying harder.

Ch. 4, p. 86–88; Ch. 17, p. 367–368
Category B

The Biomechanics & Physics Priors

The “Quiet Feet” Prior

The false prior

Keep your feet quiet, stay grounded, and coil against a stable lower body.

The reality

Suppressing lower-body ground force kills power. Tour players generate over 200% of body weight in vertical ground force and lift and replant the lead heel to free the hips and shallow the shaft.

Ch. 9, p. 207–209

The Flexibility Fallacy Prior

The false prior

Early extension is caused by a lack of hip or hamstring flexibility.

The reality

Early extension is an intelligent survival mechanism — when the delivery is steep, the brain senses the shaft is about to slam into the turf and stands the body up to create clearance. Fix the shallow plane and it disappears. Prove it yourself: swing a pencil instead of a club, and the early extension dissolves.

Ch. 10

The Textbook Position Prior

The false prior

There's one universal, perfect mechanical swing model that guarantees good shots.

The reality

Motor equivalence — different movement strategies (Furyk, Norman, Scheffler) produce identical impact conditions. The ball only responds to path, face, contact, and low point, not setup aesthetics.

Ch. 6, p. 121–123

The Calibration & Impact Priors

The “Fix Impact With Mechanics” Prior

The false prior

When you hit a slice or a shank, you need to adjust your internal swing positions.

The reality

Ball-flight issues get solved with external cues and Impact Opposites, not mechanical tweaks. Mechanics fix the swing from the inside; calibration fixes impact from the outside — they aren't interchangeable.

Ch. 3, p. 52; Ch. 7, p. 171–172

The Straight-Ball Prior

The false prior

Good golfers learn to hit it straight first, then learn to curve it.

The reality

Straight is a narrow, fragile line. Before you try to hit it straight, you should learn to correct bias — this is the heart of calibration. The best players learn to curve the ball both ways on purpose; owning both edges of the range is how the brain finds and calibrates the middle.

Ch. 1, p. 11; Ch. 5, p. 110–111

The Stock-Shot Monoculture

The false prior

Amateurs shouldn't practice shaping shots — they should stick to one stock swing.

The reality

Practicing shot-shaping is practicing calibration. Deliberately hitting fades and draws widens the skill band, sharpens the feel for neutral, and gives real emergency options on the course.

Ch. 7, p. 145–146; Ch. 15, p. 311–312

The Remedial Calibration Fallacy

The false prior

Calibration drills are a temporary phase you put away once your swing is fixed.

The reality

Bias drift is a permanent threat — path and face angle drift by single degrees over a season without you noticing. Calibration is a lifelong, two-minute daily warm-up, not a phase.

Ch. 7, p. 171–172
